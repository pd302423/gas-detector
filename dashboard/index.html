The CPCB AQI is computed from PM2.5, PM10, O₃, NO₂, SO₂, CO, NH₃ and Pb, averaged over 8 or 24 hours by reference-grade monitors. A single MQ-2 measures none of them. The index on the left borrows CPCB's band names and colours to make a sensor reading legible — the scale is CPCB's, the numbers are this project's.

Exposure limits in use

Published occupational values, and where this sensor's thresholds come from
GasHazard8-h limitCeiling / IDLH LELAlarms at

Combustibles alarm at 10% of the Lower Explosive Limit, the standard industrial convention — it leaves a factor-of-ten margin before the atmosphere can ignite. Toxics alarm on health limits instead. Permissible exposure time uses the time-weighted-average relation minutes = 480 × limit ÷ concentration, which is occupational planning arithmetic and not an evacuation instrument.

The divider is not optional

ESP8266 build only

The MQ heater needs a real 5 V, so its analog output swings the full 0–5 V. The ESP8266's A0 tolerates about 3.3 V on a NodeMCU board (and only 1.0 V on the bare ESP-12E pin). Connecting them directly damages the ADC.

68 kΩ / 100 kΩ scales 5.00 V down to 2.98 V. That is why ADC_FULLSCALE_VOLTS is 5.54 in config.h. Different resistors? Recompute as 3.3 / (R2 / (R1 + R2)).

The Arduino Uno build needs none of this — it is 5 V throughout, which is why it is the safer first build.

Your LCD, decoded

16 parallel pins in, 4 I²C pins out
PinLabelFunction

Those sixteen pins are a standard HD44780 16×2 character LCD. The four-pin module you soldered on is a PCF8574 I²C backpack — it drives all sixteen for you over two wires.

That was the right call. Driving the panel directly in 4-bit mode costs six GPIOs, and an ESP-12E does not have six to spare once the sensor, buzzer and LED are attached.

Its address is 0x27 (PCF8574T) or 0x3F (PCF8574AT). The firmware probes both, so you never need to know which you have.

Blank screen or two rows of solid blocks is almost always contrast. Turn the blue trimmer on the back of the backpack before suspecting anything else.

Running this yourself

Four steps to live readings.

If the detector is already flashed and calibrated, this is the whole procedure. First-time setup is below it.

  1. Plug the Uno into this computer

    One USB cable powers the board and carries the data. It must be a data cable — many charging cables have no data lines and the port simply never appears.

  2. Close the Arduino IDE's Serial Monitor

    Only one program can hold a serial port at a time. The Serial Monitor still holding it is the single most common reason the next step fails.

  3. Open this page in Chrome or Edge, on a computer

    Reading a USB port needs the Web Serial API, which Firefox and Safari do not have and no mobile browser has. The page must also be on https or localhost — a file opened from disk cannot touch a port.

  4. Click Connect detector and choose the port

    The pill in the header turns to USB · live and every tab fills with real data. If the button is not visible, the Live tab says which of the conditions above is missing.

Connecting resets the board — that is unavoidable with USB serial. The firmware detects that power never actually dropped and settles in 8 seconds instead of restarting the full 180-second heater warm-up.

First-time setup

From a box of parts to a working detector

  1. Wire it — ten wires, no resistors

    MQ-135 VCC→5V GND→GND AOUT→A0 DOUT→D7; LCD backpack VCC→5V GND→GND SDA→A4 SCL→A5; buzzer long leg →D8, short leg →GND. The alarm LED is the Uno's own pin-13 one — nothing to wire. The Wiring tab shows every connection; tap a wire to see what it carries.

  2. Check the sensor actually works

    Upload sensor_check/sensor_check.ino first. It needs only three wires and ignores the LCD, so it still reports the truth if the display is dead. Three things prove the sensor is alive: it gets warm within a minute, the reading is plausible rather than zero or pinned, and it moves when you wave hand sanitiser at it. The last one is the one that matters — a dead sensing layer still reads a convincing steady number.

  3. Install the one library and flash the firmware

    Arduino IDE → Tools → Manage Libraries → install LiquidCrystal I2C by Frank de Brabander. Open gas_detector_uno/gas_detector_uno.ino, confirm MQ_SELECT matches the number on your sensor's can, set BUZZER_PASSIVE (steady tone from DC = 0, single click = 1), and upload.

  4. Burn it in for 24–48 hours

    A new MQ sensor's baseline drifts for that long. Leave it powered overnight, twice. Readings taken before it settles do not mean anything, and no amount of calibration fixes that.

  5. Calibrate R₀ in clean air

    Take it outdoors — not the kitchen, not a room where anyone has cooked or used sanitiser. Serial Monitor at 115200, send c. That measures the sensor's clean-air resistance and stores it in EEPROM, where it survives being unplugged. Every number this dashboard shows is a ratio against it, so calibrating in a contaminated room makes everything downstream quietly wrong. Send r any time for raw diagnostics.

Air Quality Index coverage

The eight pollutants CPCB's index is computed from, and how many this build can actually measure

The ceiling is three of eight, and that is the honest end of it. Ozone, nitrogen dioxide and sulphur dioxide need UV-photometric, chemiluminescence and UV-fluorescence analysers; lead needs laboratory analysis of filter samples. Those four cost more than every other part of this project combined, and no hobby sensor substitutes for them. What v3 does buy is PM2.5 and PM10 — the two that dominate the index in most Indian cities.

Making it trustworthy

Engineering work, not new sensors
ImprovementProblem it solvesPriority

Sensor failure detection is the single most important item on this page. Right now an open circuit on the sensor reads as infinite resistance — which the maths interprets as perfectly clean air. A detector that reports "all clear" because it is broken is worse than no detector, and this build cannot currently tell the difference. Checking heater continuity would catch most of it.

The one that changes what the instrument is

An array, and a classifier

Everything else on this page adds a measurement. This one removes the limitation the whole project is built around: that a single sensor produces one number and therefore cannot name a gas.

Six MQ sensors with different dopings — 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 and 135 — respond to the same gas by different ratios. Butane might drive the MQ-6 hard and the MQ-3 barely at all; ethanol does the reverse. No single sensor can tell them apart, but the pattern across six of them is gas-specific. That pattern is a six-dimensional vector, and separating vectors into labelled classes is exactly what a classifier does.

StepWhat you buildThe detail that bites
1Wire six MQ sensors The Uno has six analog pins and A4/A5 are the I²C bus, so only four are free. You need a CD74HC4067 16-channel analog multiplexer, or an ADS1115 4-channel I²C ADC — which also gets you 16-bit resolution instead of the Uno's 10-bit.
2Calibrate each R₀ separately Six independent clean-air baselines, six EEPROM slots. Every sensor also needs its own 24–48 h burn-in.
3Collect labelled samples The real work. Expose the array to each target gas repeatedly, at varying concentrations and distances, logging all six Rs/R₀ values per sample. A few hundred samples per gas. The CSV export already on the System tab is where this starts.
4Train a classifier k-nearest-neighbours first — it needs no training step, runs on the Arduino from a stored table, and is easy to explain to a judge. Move to a small neural net only if k-NN's accuracy plateaus.
5Report identity with confidence Report the distance to the nearest class, not just the label. A sample far from every trained class is an unknown gas — and saying so is the honest answer, exactly as this version says "at most one of these rows is true".

Normalise each sensor to its own Rs/R₀ before classifying, never raw resistance — absolute resistance varies by a factor of two between two units of the same part number, so a classifier trained on raw ohms learns your specific sensors rather than the gases.

Read this before trusting a number

One sensor cannot name the gas.

An MQ sensor is a heated tin-dioxide bead. Its resistance falls when any reducing gas lands on the surface. The output is one number — not a spectrum, not a fingerprint.

So LPG at 500 ppm, ethanol vapour at 200 ppm, and cigarette smoke can all produce an identical reading. Every gas row on the Live tab is that same single measurement re-interpreted: "if this were LPG, it would be this much." At most one row is physically true, and the sensor does not know which.

What this system honestly is: a reducing-gas alarm with an estimated concentration and a hazard classification. That is what a certified kitchen gas alarm does, and it is genuinely useful. What it is not is a gas identifier.

If you need real identification

ApproachMechanismEffort
Sensor array + classifier Four to six different MQ types read at once. Each has a different sensitivity ratio to each gas, so the pattern across the array is gas-specific. Train k-NN or a small net on labelled samples. Moderate — this is the competition-grade version
Heater cycling Drive one sensor's heater between two temperatures (MQ-7: 5 V/60 s then 1.4 V/90 s). Selectivity shifts with temperature, giving two semi-independent readings from one part. No extra hardware, needs PWM and timing discipline
NDIR Infrared absorption at a gas-specific wavelength. Genuinely selective. MH-Z19 for CO₂. Easy — but one gas per sensor
Electrochemical cell A gas-specific reaction produces a current proportional to concentration. Factory-calibrated in real ppm. Easy, higher cost per gas
PID / GC / spectrometry Actual analytical chemistry. Separates and identifies. Lab equipment

Other things that move the reading

Humidity and temperature shift the baseline on their own. A muggy afternoon can look like a small leak.

Age. The heater and the sensing layer drift over months. Recalibrate seasonally.

Burn-in. A brand-new sensor drifts for 24–48 hours of continuous power. Readings before that are meaningless.

The curve constants are least-squares fits to a graph printed in a datasheet PDF. Order-of-magnitude accurate — fine for an alarm, not for reporting concentrations as data.

Not a life-safety device

This build has no self-test, no sensor-failure detection, no battery backup, and no certification. Its calibration traces back to a curve fit, not a reference gas.

For actual protection of a home with an LPG cylinder, buy a BIS or CE certified alarm. Build this one to understand how that one works — and keep the certified one on the wall.
